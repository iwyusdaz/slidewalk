Part 1 — Why WCAG belongs to you

  • First: the mental model (POUR)
  • Then: the everyday checklist

Speaker notes

Signpost the two halves of the session. Part 1 is the mental model; Part 2 is the practical checklist people can use tomorrow.

Accessibility is part of your job

  • The words you write become what screen readers speak
  • Missing alt text, vague links and walls of text are content decisions, not code bugs
  • Retrofitting is expensive; writing it right the first time is nearly free
  • Legal reality: public-sector bodies (and many private ones) must meet WCAG 2.1 AA

Speaker notes

Ask the room who they think "owns" accessibility. Most people point at developers. Walk through the three examples on the slide — every one of them is a writing or publishing decision. Developers can only make accessible what content gives them. A screen reader can only read the words that were written.

What WCAG is — and isn't

  • WCAG = Web Content Accessibility Guidelines, published by the W3C
  • Three conformance levels: A (must), AA (should, and usually the legal bar), AAA (aspirational)
  • It is technology-neutral: principles and testable criteria, not a code recipe
  • Meeting AA is a floor, not a guarantee — real users still need to be listened to

Speaker notes

Keep this short — it is orientation, not legal training. Emphasise that WCAG is technology-neutral: it says "provide text alternatives", not "use this attribute". The pass/fail line for most organisations is AA; AAA is aspirational for most content. Be honest that meeting WCAG does not guarantee a good experience for every disabled user — it is a floor, not a ceiling.

The four POUR principles

  • Perceivable — can everyone sense it?
  • Operable — can everyone use it?
  • Understandable — does it make sense?
  • Robust — can machines read it?

Speaker notes

This is the backbone of the whole session — spend time here. Read the diagram aloud and give a one-line plain-language version of each principle. Tell learners the next four slides take one principle each, and that the checkpoint right after will ask them to recall the four from memory. Encourage them to translate each principle into a question they would ask about their own pages. The plain-language questions on the diagram are the ones learners should memorise.

Perceivable — alternatives you provide

  • Alt text for meaningful images; empty alt for decorative ones
  • Captions for video; transcripts for audio
  • "chart.png" is not alt text — describe what the reader would miss
  • Test: if the image vanished, what information would be gone? That is your alt text

Speaker notes

Give the fail-forward example: an image of a chart with alt text "chart.png" helps nobody. The test for alt text: close your eyes and have someone read the page — would you get the same information? Mention that decorative images should get empty alt text so screen readers skip them, which is a decision only the author can make. Captions benefit far more people than deaf users: people in open offices, on trains, with sound muted.

Operable — everything works by keyboard

  • Some users cannot use a mouse — tremors, RSI, screen-reader or switch users
  • If it works by click, it must work by keyboard (Tab, Enter, arrows)
  • Visible focus outline is a feature, not a design flaw
  • Hover-only menus and click-only widgets are the usual suspects

Speaker notes

Ask people to unplug their mouse for thirty seconds and try their own site — this lands harder than any explanation. The common content-side keyboard killers are components that only respond to click and links hidden behind hover-only menus. Also mention: never remove the focus outline because it "looks ugly"; visible focus is how keyboard users know where they are.

Understandable — plain language, predictable pages

  • Short sentences, common words, expand acronyms on first use
  • Say the same thing the same way everywhere (labels, buttons, menus)
  • Consistent navigation helps people who rely on memory and habit
  • Plain language helps everyone — including your stressed, distracted expert users

Speaker notes

Read a sentence from your own organisation's site that fails the plain-language test and fix it live if you can. Predictability means menus and terms stay the same across pages — consistency is an editorial job. Explain that this principle exists largely for people with cognitive and learning disabilities, the largest disability group that WCAG's cognitive criteria serve.

Robust — markup assistive tech can read

  • Use real headings, real lists, real tables — not bold text that looks like them
  • Meaning you write becomes structure assistive tech navigates by
  • Fake structure (visual-only headings, space-formatted tables) is invisible to machines
  • Agree with your developers on what "good content" hands over

Speaker notes

This is the principle authors share with developers, so frame it as a collaboration contract: authors supply meaning, developers supply markup that preserves it. The classic failure is a visually bold line that is not a real heading — it looks structured but screen readers see a paragraph. Same for fake tables made of tabs and spaces in a PDF. If the machine cannot see the structure, no amount of styling helps.

Part 2 — Your everyday checklist

  • Four checks, five minutes, every page

Speaker notes

Signpost: four checks, all doable in under five minutes per page.

Alt text that actually works

  • Informative image → describe the information it carries
  • Decorative image → alt="" so screen readers skip it
  • Image inside a link → alt text is the action ("Search", not "icon")
  • Never stuff keywords, never leave "image1.jpg"

Speaker notes

Walk the three cases. Informative images: describe the information, not the picture. Decorative: empty alt, and let them go. Functional (an image inside a link or button): the alt text is the action — "Search", not "magnifying glass icon". Warn about the two classic failures: keyword-stuffed alt text for SEO, and file names pasted in as alt.

Contrast — text everyone can read

  • Body text: at least 4.5:1 against its background
  • Large text (18pt+ or 14pt bold): at least 3:1
  • Light grey body text in templates is the most common failure
  • Free tools: WebAIM contrast checker, or your browser's DevTools

Speaker notes

Show the numbers, then show a real contrast checker (the WebAIM contrast checker or the browser DevTools) on a fail and a pass. Mention low-contrast grey-on-white body text as the most common offender in corporate templates. Also flag that light-grey placeholder text in forms usually fails too.

Links, headings and structure

  • Link text must make sense out of context — screen readers skim by link list
  • "Click here" and "read more" fail that test; name the destination instead
  • Headings in order (h1 → h2 → h3), used for structure, not for font size
  • Many screen-reader users navigate entirely by headings

Speaker notes

The classic test: read only the links on a page aloud — "click here", "read more", "learn more" tells a screen-reader user nothing, because many of them skim by link list. For headings: many screen-reader users navigate by heading list, so heading levels must be real and in order. Warn against skipping levels for visual size — that is what CSS is for.

Check your work before you publish

  • Run an automated check (axe DevTools, WAVE) — it catches roughly a third of issues
  • Tab through the page without a mouse
  • Read your alt text aloud: would you get the same information?
  • Automated passes are a floor, not a certificate — real users are the real test

Speaker notes

Frame this as the habit that makes everything stick. Automated tools catch about a third of issues — they find missing alt text and contrast failures, but cannot judge whether alt text is good. The keyboard pass catches what machines miss. Close with the honest framing: WCAG AA is the floor; listening to disabled users is how you get past it.

Wrap-up — your routine and where to go next

  • Ask the four POUR questions about every page you publish
  • Run the five-minute pre-publish check
  • Explore the WCAG quick reference: w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/quickref
  • Bring one of your own pages next time — we will check it together

Speaker notes

Recap in one breath: perceivable, operable, understandable, robust — four questions you can ask about any page. Point people at the WCAG 2.1 quick reference (w3.org) and their organisation's accessibility policy. Thank the room; invite them to bring a real page to the next session and run the checklist on it together.
